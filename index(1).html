Before You Fly

First things first

Load Suica to your iPhone Wallet before landing — or buy a physical Welcome Suica card at Haneda the moment you clear customs. This one card pays for every train, subway, bus, and convenience store all week. Load ¥5,000 (~$35) each to start. Bring cash — Japan is still cash-heavy at small restaurants and markets. Withdraw yen from any 7-Eleven ATM (always accepts foreign cards).

Shimokitazawa — bohemian Tokyo
Take the Odakyu Line from Shinjuku (or Keio Inokashira from Shibuya) to Shimokitazawa. Cat Street, Harajuku — the anti-tourist shopping street
Ride the Odakyu or Keio Line to Harajuku Station, then walk 5 minutes to Cat Street (キャットストリート) — an informal pedestrian lane running parallel to the flashy Omotesando boulevard, but completely different in character. No luxury brands here. Instead: independent vintage shops, tiny select boutiques, concept stores, street food carts, and cafés you won't find anywhere else. The street has a relaxed, unhurried energy that's rare for central Tokyo. Browse Beams or United Arrows side alleys, duck into a vintage shop, pick up a coffee. This is the side of Harajuku that fashion-forward Tokyo actually inhabits — not the Takeshita Street costume chaos.

Sento etiquette — read this

Undress fully in the changing room, store clothes in the basket or locker. Take a small towel and washbasin, find a shower stall, wash every inch of yourself before entering the shared tub. Keep your towel folded on your head or beside the bath — never in the water. Speak quietly. Don't stare. Enjoy.

Local Rules

  • 1
    Don't tip. Ever. Anywhere. Tipping is considered rude in Japan — it implies the server doesn't earn enough or that you feel superior. Just say gochisousama deshita when you leave (thank you for the meal).
  • 2
    Eat while walking is frowned upon in most places, except at street stalls where it's expected. If you buy something from a stall, eat it right there.
  • 3
    On the subway: be quiet, no phone calls, give up your seat. Nobody talks. Stand on the left of escalators (Tokyo convention) so walkers can pass on the right.
  • 4
    Cash is king at small restaurants, market stalls, and shrines. Always carry ¥5,000–10,000 (~$35–69) on you. Convenience stores and most chain restaurants take cards and Suica.
  • 5
    Standing at the counter at a sushi bar, ramen counter, or tonkatsu joint is completely normal and often results in better food. Don't wait for a table if there are counter seats.
  • 6
    The combini is your friend. Lawson, 7-Eleven, and Family Mart sell genuinely good food, cheap hot coffee, and anything you forgot to pack. The onigiri here is better than sandwiches at most airports anywhere in the world.
  • 7
    At restaurants with no English menu: point confidently at what looks good, say kore (this one), hold up fingers for quantity. Smiling and genuine curiosity go a long way. Most chefs are quietly delighted a foreigner is eating their food.
